SECTION A: INTRODUCTION (2%)



Language plays a central role in shaping cultural identity, community belonging, and social

integration. In multilingual countries such as Canada - where over 200 languages are spoken -

understanding whether individuals maintain their mother tongue across generations provides

valuable insight into cultural continuity and demographic change. This project investigates

patterns of language retention in Canada, focusing on the extent to which individuals

continue to speak their mother tongue at home and how this varies across provinces, age

groups, and language communities. Our main objective is to measure and visualize mother

tongue retention using reliable national data, then interpret the patterns within Canada’s

unique linguistic landscape.


Language retention is especially important in Canada because of its official bilingualism

policies, long  history  of immigration, and strong Indigenous and immigrant language

communities. The 2021 Census shows that millions of Canadians report a mother tongue

other than English and French, yet not all continue to use that language most often at home.

Retention rates can reflect cultural attachment, generational shifts, government language

policies, and the influence of dominant languages within specific regions. For example,

Quebec’s French-language protections may encourage different retention outcomes compared

to provinces where English  is  the overwhelming majority language. Similarly, newer

immigrant groups may have higher retention due to strong community networks, while

third-generation speakers often shift toward English or French. These dynamics make Canada

an ideal case study for analyzing how languages survive - or fade - over time.


To explore these issues, we use Statistics Canada Table 98-10-0169-01 from the 2021 Census,

a comprehensive dataset that reports mother tongues, first official language spoken, and

languages spoken at home across all provinces and territories. Using this dataset allows us to

calculate  retention  rates,  compare  patterns  across  regions, and  identify  generational

differences with precision and consistency. By analyzing this publicly available census data,

we aim to uncover meaningful trends in how Canadians maintain their linguistic heritage and

what social, cultural, or regional factors may be influencing these outcomes.

Indigenous language retention in Canada. Some regions show relatively strong continuity of

speakers but only about five in “Neither English nor French” category, giving a retention rate

of just 0.3%, and Prince Edward Island has 45 Indigenous speakers with no recorded cases in

this category, indicating no measurable retention under our definition.



     When these patterns are considered alongside the results for English, French and

non-official languages presented in Question 2, Indigenous mother tongues generally appear

mother tongue languages at home, knowing the official Canadian languages – English and

French – will be taught and learned naturally in school and society. The only provinces that

do not fit into this blanket statement are Quebec and New Brunswick. This makes sense as

these provinces have the two highest percentages of French speakers in Canada.
