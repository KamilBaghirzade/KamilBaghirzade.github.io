Desjardins 2019 Data Breach:
How Ineffective Security Led To The Largest Banking Data Breach
In Canadian History

1Background:

Desjardins is a Canadian financial corporation that serves millions of Canadians holding
critical banking information and personal information (social insurance numbers, legal
names, emails, addresses). In 2019, it came to light that up to 9.7 million Canadians had their
privacy compromised due to the actions of an employee working under Desjardins. This
report was heavily influenced by the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada's (OPC)
investigation made on December 14, 2020.

Two types of people were affected; Desjardins members (shareholders, direct clients) and
Desjardins clients (affiliated with a Desjardins member)

How the information was acquired:
    ●​  Desjardin held its data in two settings, a ‘confidential’ and ‘non-confidential’.
        Typically, non-confidential was accessible to all employees while confidential had
       limited access.
    ●​ Those with authorization could access the confidential information and transfer the
       data to their own ‘user’ folder which would be contained in a shared directory
       accessible to all employees.
    ●​ The malicious employee was not authorized to handle or access the confidential data,
       but with this system, they were able to copy the data from their colleagues with
       authorization.
    ●​ They would then save the data on their own ‘user’ folder where they would then
       transfer the information onto a USB drive and could then misuse or disclose the data
       without authorization.
    ●​ The employee violated their employer contract on confidentiality.

Stakeholders & Power:

This case has several key stakeholders with different levels of power, benefit, and risk. In
terms of strict power, it is up to the OPC to recommend changes to Desjardins internal
security but most of the responsibility (and in this case power), lies with Desjardins. In terms
of individuals who held little to no power, it would be the Desjardins members and clients

Who Benefits
The malicious employee and their third parties benefited as the employee could make tens of
thousands of dollars off the stolen identities, and those who purchased or obtained it could
manipulate their customers or use them for their own personal benefit.

Who Carries Risk
The risk lies on not only Desjardins but all Desjardins members and clients affected by the
data breach. Years later in 2024, additional suspects were arrested in relation to the data
breach and when the public was asked how they felt, there were mixed feelings. Some
responded how they still feel unprotected, specifically, they are concerned that Desjardins
holds too much information and aren’t confident that they’ve done enough to protect it.
Alternatively, some were confident in Desjardins and trusted them, citing how this could’ve
happened to any bank and the new implementations reduce the risk of another breach.

Who Had Decision Authority
Desjardins held the decision authority while their clients had little to do with their own data.
Desjardin was responsible for the protection / safeguard of their clients data and was
supposed to only hold such data for its intended purpose (not indefinitely). So Desjardins
bears primary accountability for its failure to uphold its clients' data.

Who Lacked A Voice
The public and businesses affected were not consulted by the Canadian Government or
Desjardins during the investigation and compiling of the report. Based on what the
government has released, their methodology of obtaining information and compiling their
report was from “consult(ing) various open sources and reviewed the information provided
by Desjardins”4. During the course of the 7700+ word investigation, the word ‘public’ or
‘people’ is not mentioned once either in the context of consultation or potential solutions.5
Instead, they only refer to the Desjardin clients and members affected in the form of
compensation and future protection rather than how their data will be re-secured now.

Through this case, Desjardins and its clients held a disproportionate share of the risks and
benefits. Desjardins had little to gain by retaining its clients' sensitive data, while its clients
had the most to lose in the result of a data breach. Not only that, the little mention of its
clients themselves seems as though Desjardins and the OPC did not do a thorough
investigation or response plan to address the most affected members of the data breach.

Ethical Analysis:

Quebec’s version of PIPEDA (Act Respecting the Protection of Personal Information in the Private
Sector) was considered but for businesses that operate on the federal level (including banks
such as Desjardins) regular PIPEDA still would apply.

The PIPEDA topics violated by Desjardins (as identified by the OPC) are:
     1.​ Accountability
     2.​ Limiting Use, Retention, and Disclosure of Data
     3.​ Implementing Safeguards


1. Accountability
Accountability under PIPEDA entails that “an organization (Desjardins) is responsible for
the personal information under its control”. Desjardins through this internal data breach is
still responsible as it was under their control, safeguarded under their security measures.
Specifically, Desjardins violated principles 4.1.3, 4.1.4(a), and 4.1.4(c). These three principles
describe how Desjardins is responsible for not just the information, but its transfer as well
(4.1.3). In addition, they are also responsible for keeping staff trained in the implemented
procedures to safely handle the data (4.1.4). As this data breach was internal through a
mismanagement of its transfer or information between departments by its own staff shows
how the principles above were violated.

The accountability does not fall onto the malicious employee alone as they were only enabled
by the faulty security system Desjardin had in place. Desjardins remained accountable
because it was the party that collected, stored, and managed the data. Ethically under
PIPEDA, an organization cannot shift blame entirely onto one employee when it failed to
build a system capable of preventing or detecting foreseeable misuse earlier.

2. Limiting Use, Retention, and Disclosure of Data
During the investigation conducted by the OPC, they found that Desjardins were in the
process of implementing retention periods (how long they could keep the data). Current
policies were also not fully completed which caused some sensitive data that was considered
‘inactive’ to be visible decades after they were collected.6 This is in direct violation of

PIPEDA Principle 4.5.3 which outlines that procedures should be implemented to destroy,
erase, or anonymize data that isn’t required for its intended purpose (the inactive accounts).
Of the 9.7 million individuals whose data was collected, 3.9 million7 of them were believed to
be considered inactive. That is nearly 4 million individuals whose data should not have been
in Desjardins system regardless of the breach. This was negligent on the part of Desjardins
as if they wanted to keep a record of the information, they at the very least should have
anonymised it.

Desjardins also had an ethical duty to limit how much sensitive information was available,
how long it was kept, and who could access it. By allowing confidential and non-confidential
data to be handled in a way that increased exposure, the organization increased the scale of
harm when the breach happened.

3. Implementing Safeguards
As mentioned previously, the architecture of Desjardins system was built in a way that could
be exploited. An employee without proper authorization was able to access information not
normally accessible for their security clearance which directly violates PIPEDA Principle 7 -
Safeguards. More specifically, its method to protect personal information (PIPEDA Principle
4.7.3)8. The specific violation would be "organizational measures, for example, security
clearance and limiting access on a ‘need-to-know’ basis” (Principle 4.7.3(b)). Unauthorized
personnel accessing information they are not privy to is in direct violation of this principle
which Desjardins violated.

One of Desjardins’ largest blunders was that it did not implement / put into practice the
standard procedures they should’ve done. They had numerous policies and procedures that if
implemented properly, would have never allowed for the information to be either identifiable
or even accessible to unauthorised personnel.

An additional violation that was briefly mentioned by the OPC was PIPEDA Principle 4.7.4
which discusses employee awareness of confidentiality of personal information. Earlier in
the report, it was stated that Desjardins work environment was one of trust. The malicious
employee was seen as ‘a skilled and high performing employee’. While this is good for a
workplace as both we and OPC think, it is too idealistic for every employee to follow the
rules.

Trade-Offs:

This case involves several trade-offs that cannot be fully resolved, even with stronger
reforms.

First, Desjardins needed to make personal data accessible enough for employees to carry out
legitimate banking, customer-service, and administrative functions, but increasing
accessibility also increased the risk of insider misuse. In a large financial institution, some
employee access is necessary for normal service delivery. But in the case of Desjardins, they
relied too heavily on the supposed ‘climate’ of trust’ rather than preparing and preventing
the worst case scenario. Tighter access controls and stricter monitoring on data transfers
would reduce the chance of another breach, yet these measures may also slow down daily
operations and make legitimate work less efficient.

Second, retaining large amounts of personal information can support fraud prevention,
service continuity, and business operations, but keeping sensitive data for too long increases
the scale of harm if a breach occurs. Inactive accounts, while may be essential in
record-keeping, should not have been transferred between members of the marketing
department as we don’t see why that would be needed if they are not ongoing businesses or
accounts. This is especially important in this case because inactive individuals’ data was still
being retained, which meant the breach affected people whose information may no longer
have been needed for ongoing business purposes.

Third, stronger monitoring of employees may improve accountability and detection of
suspicious activity, but it also creates concerns about workplace surveillance, trust, and
employee autonomy. As mentioned before, the level of camaraderie between the Desjardins
marketing department, while commendable, left too many errors and breaches in procedure
which enabled a malicious employee to exploit their lack of vigilance.

Finally, compensation and credit-monitoring measures can help reduce some of the harms
suffered by affected individuals, but they cannot fully reverse the long-term privacy and
identity-theft risks once the information has already been exposed. In other words,
post-breach support can reduce damage, but it cannot fully restore the privacy that was lost.9

Alternatives & Mitigation:

Several realistic changes could have reduced the likelihood and impact of this breach.
Desjardins should have kept confidential and non-confidential data strictly separate, limited
employee access to only what was necessary for their role, and monitored unusual copying or
transfer activity more closely. This is especially important because the OPC found that
confidential information could be transferred into shared user folders that were accessible
more broadly than they should have been, which increased the risk of unauthorized internal
access. It also should have conducted stronger audits, reviewed access permissions regularly,
and retained sensitive personal information for shorter periods of time. The OPC also found
that Desjardins had not fully implemented appropriate retention practices, which meant
some inactive individuals’ data remained in the system longer than necessary. After the
breach, mitigation should not have focused only on compensation, but also on long-term
support such as fraud recovery, ongoing credit protection, and clearer communication with
affected individuals. While Desjardins did set some compensation in place for those affected,
we feel as though it's not doing enough to recover the data that was lost. These changes
would not remove insider risk completely, but they would have made the breach harder to
carry out and easier to detect earlier.

The OPC’s response mainly addresses the problem of weak internal safeguards at
Desjardins. Its recommendations pushed Desjardins to strengthen access controls, improve
monitoring, tighten retention practices, and increase accountability for how sensitive
personal information is handled. These changes help reduce the risk of another similar
insider breach by making unauthorized access and large-scale data extraction harder to carry
out and easier to detect. They also provide some support to affected individuals through
compensation, credit monitoring, and other protection measures, which helps address part
of the immediate harm caused by the breach. More specifically, the OPC found
contraventions related to accountability, safeguards, and retention, so its response was
mainly directed at correcting those internal weaknesses.

Recommendations:

Desjardins should implement stricter access controls by enforcing a true “need-to-know”
system where employees can only access data required for their specific role. Confidential
information should be kept strictly separate from general employee directories, and unusual
copying, downloading, or transfer activity should be monitored more closely. Desjardins

should also shorten retention periods for inactive or unnecessary personal data and improve
regular audits of internal data-handling practices. At the same time, regulators such as the
OPC should continue external oversight to ensure that these safeguards are not only written
in policy, but actually followed in practice. In addition to technical reforms, Desjardins
should provide long-term support for affected individuals through continued credit
protection, fraud assistance, and transparent communication about ongoing risks.

Residual Risks:

Although these changes and recommendations may improve the security of Desjardins’
internal system, this doesn’t mean that all the data that had been shared prior has
disappeared. It is still unknown as to how many other people acquired portions of the stolen
information, but that still lies with Desjardins’ failure to implement safeguards and holding
sensitive information for longer than required. The fault also lies with Desjardins and the
OPC for failing to come up with a solution to recover or re-anonymize the data that had been
leaked.

The aftermath of this can still be felt today as more and more suspects are arrested in
connection to this case.10 Recently in 2025, there was a resurgence of the stolen information
circulating the internet, specifically the dark web.11 This shows that the effects of the
Desjardins 2019 data breach, while mitigated now, still has not been fully resolved.
