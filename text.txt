Problem Description

Online rental scams are a serious real-world problem because they target people when
they are already under pressure. In a competitive housing market, a listing that appears
cheap, available immediately, and easy to secure can feel like a lucky opportunity.
Scammers exploit that pressure by posting fake or misleading rental advertisements,
pretending to be landlords, and asking victims to send deposits or personal information
before the renter has safely verified the property.

Rental scams often imitate legitimate rental behaviour. A scammer may copy photos
from another website, use the address of a real property, offer a rent price that is
unusually low for the area, claim to be unavailable for an in-person viewing, and ask for
payment before a lease is properly signed. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission warns
that duplicate advertisements, pressure to send money before viewing a property, and
unusually low prices can all be warning signs of a rental scam [1]. The FBI also warns
renters not to send funds to unknown people, not to pay for a property they have not
seen, and to confirm the landlord’s identity through records or other verification methods
[2]. Competition Bureau Canada gives similar warnings, explaining that scammers may
post attractive listings with low prices, use copied photos, and claim to be outside the
country or unable to meet in person [3].

Current solutions are useful but incomplete. Rental platforms often provide report
buttons, manual moderation, identity checks, and user education pages. Governments
and police agencies also publish scam warnings. The RCMP explains that some rental
scams involve fake property advertisements, demands for deposits without viewing the
property, or requests for personal information and e-transfer before meeting in person
[4]. These warnings help, but they place most of the burden on renters. A student,
newcomer, or young worker may not know local rent prices or how to verify a landlord.
Manual moderation also does not scale well when scams can be reposted across
websites and social media groups.

The opportunity is not to replace human judgment but to support it earlier. A useful
system would identify suspicious rental listings before a renter sends money or shares
sensitive information. This does not require artificial general intelligence. It requires a
specialized AI tool that combines patterns from data with clear rules about known scam
signals. Some parts of the problem are rule-based, such as a request for a deposit
before a viewing. Other parts are pattern-based, such as repeated persuasion
strategies, copied phrasing, unrealistic prices, or suspicious contact behaviour. Van der
Zee, Clayton, and Anderson found that fraudulent rental advertisers often try to
persuade victims to transfer money without seeing the property [5].

This is the problem RentSafe AI aims to solve. Its goal is to give renters and rental
platforms an early warning system for likely fake listings. The system would not label a
listing as definitely fraudulent. Instead, it would produce a risk score, explain the main
reasons for that score, and recommend verification before any payment is made.

Proposed Solution

RentSafe AI would be a hybrid AI system that analyzes online rental listings and
classifies them as low risk, medium risk, or high risk. The machine learning component
would learn patterns from legitimate and fraudulent listings, while the symbolic
component would apply explicit rules based on known scam warning signs. The hybrid
design is important because a purely rule-based system may miss new scam patterns,
while a purely machine-learning system may be less transparent and harder to trust.

The input would be a rental listing. A listing could include rent price, location, number of
bedrooms, text description, photos, posting date, poster account information, contact
method, and message content. The system would convert these details into feature
vectors. Numerical features could include rent price, number of images, account age,
number of similar listings by the same account, and the difference between the listed
price and estimated local market price. Text features could include phrases such as
“urgent,” “send deposit now,” “out of country,” “no viewing possible,” or “wire transfer.”
Image features could include whether the same photo appears in multiple listings or
whether the photos seem inconsistent with the stated location.

For the machine learning component, the main task would be supervised classification.
The training data would consist of listings labelled as legitimate, suspicious, or
confirmed scam. At scale, data could come from rental-platform partnerships using user
reports, confirmed moderation outcomes, verified landlord samples,
consumer-protection examples, and research datasets. To avoid unreliable labels,
trained moderators or consumer-protection reviewers should confirm cases using
evidence such as duplicate photos, payment requests, inconsistent addresses, and
message records. A model such as logistic regression, decision trees, random forests,
or gradient-boosted trees could be used first because these models are easier to
explain than a large neural network.

The symbolic expert-system layer would encode known warning rules. The risk score
would increase if a listing asks for a deposit before viewing, refuses an in-person or live
video tour, lists rent far below similar nearby properties, reuses photos under different
names or addresses, or requests payment through unusual or irreversible methods.
These rules would not prove fraud by themselves, but they would provide clear signals
that can be explained to the user.

RentSafe AI would compare each new listing’s address, images, text, and price against
nearby rentals and known scam patterns. Using heuristics such as unusually low rent,
repeated images, or payment requests, it would prioritize suspicious cases and return a
risk score with a short explanation. Low risk would mean no strong warning signs were
found, medium risk would recommend verification, and high risk would warn the user
not to send money until human review or independent confirmation occurs. The system
would be evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and analysis of false positives and
false negatives because missed scams could harm renters, while false alarms could
unfairly affect legitimate landlords.

Challenges

Correctness and accuracy would be the most important concern because RentSafe AI
deals with decisions that can affect people financially. If the system produces a false
negative, a scam listing may appear safe and a renter could send money or personal
information. If the system produces a false positive, a legitimate landlord or renter could
be harmed because the listing may be hidden, delayed, or treated with suspicion. Both
errors matter, but false negatives may lead to direct financial loss, while false positives
may cause unfair inconvenience or reputational harm. This makes accuracy more
complicated than simply getting the highest percentage of correct predictions, because
the system must consider the real-world cost of each mistake.

This concern arises in the classification model, the rule-based system, and the data
collection process. If the training data contains mostly obvious scams, the system may
fail to detect more realistic scams. If the data comes mainly from one city or platform,
the model may not generalize well to other rental markets. A price that seems
suspiciously low in Toronto may not mean the same thing in a smaller city. The
rule-based system can also be too strict, since some legitimate landlords may be
temporarily unavailable for in-person viewings. Scammers may also change their
wording once common warning signs become known, so the system must be tested
against newer examples rather than only older scam patterns.

To tackle this issue, RentSafe AI should be a warning and review system, not an
automatic judge. The model should be trained and tested on listings from different cities,
price ranges, housing types, and platforms. Evaluation should include precision and
recall, not only overall accuracy. The system should also track false positives and false
negatives after deployment so feedback can improve future versions. Confidence
thresholds would help because a listing should only be marked high risk when there are
multiple strong warning signs.

Another way to reduce accuracy problems is to separate warnings from final actions. A
high-risk score should not automatically remove a listing unless a human moderator
confirms the issue. This matters because rental situations can have unusual but
legitimate explanations, such as a landlord travelling, a shared unit having lower rent, or
a property manager reusing similar photos. Human review would let the system warn
users early without making overconfident decisions.

Bias is also a serious concern because rental markets already involve unequal
treatment. The AI might wrongly flag listings from small landlords, immigrants, people
with imperfect English, or landlords in cheaper neighbourhoods. A model trained mostly
on polished corporate listings might treat informal writing as suspicious, while a model
trained mostly on expensive markets might treat lower rent as suspicious even when it
is normal for a smaller area. Features such as grammar, location, price, and account
age may correlate with factors that should not be used to judge honesty. If these
correlations are not checked, the system could appear technically accurate while still
treating some groups of users unfairly.

To address this, the system should be tested for uneven error rates across different
listing types and locations. The model should not rely heavily on grammar or language
quality alone. Informal writing may be a weak signal, but it should not be enough to
mark a listing high risk. More objective signals, such as duplicate photos, inconsistent
addresses, unrealistic pricing, payment-before-viewing requests, and verified user
reports, should carry more weight. High-risk results should also go to human review
rather than automatically banning a poster. The system should say that a listing has
warning signs, not that a specific person is a scammer.

Bias should also be monitored after deployment, not only during training. Even if the
original model performs well, the rental market can change over time. New scam
patterns may appear, and normal posting styles may also change. RentSafe AI should
therefore be updated using reviewed cases rather than relying forever on the original
dataset. This would help prevent the model from becoming outdated or unfairly
suspicious of legitimate listings.

Accountability matters because RentSafe AI could influence important housing
decisions. A renter might decide not to apply for a unit because the system warns them.
A rental platform might delay or remove a listing. A landlord might challenge the result.
If the system makes a mistake, it must be clear who is responsible and how the decision
can be reviewed. Without accountability, the AI could become a black-box authority that
people follow without understanding. This is especially important because users may
treat an AI-generated risk score as more objective than it really is.

To deal with accountability, RentSafe AI should keep an explanation log for each risk
score, including which signals contributed to the result and which model version made
the prediction. Users should have an appeal process if their listing is restricted. The
system should also have clear limits. It should not automatically contact landlords,
collect unnecessary personal documents, or share user data with outside parties. Its
role is to identify listing-level risk and explain it, while the final decision remains with the
renter, platform, or human reviewer.

Accountability also connects to user trust. If RentSafe AI only gives a score without
explanation, users may either ignore it completely or trust it too much. A clear
explanation helps users understand what the system found and what they should do
next. For example, instead of only saying “high risk,” the system should explain that the
listing requests payment before viewing, uses copied photos, or has a price far below
comparable rentals. This makes the AI a decision-support tool rather than a hidden
authority.

Comparison

RentSafe AI is best designed as a hybrid system because rental scam detection has
both rule-based and pattern-based parts. A symbolic AI approach is useful when
experts can describe warning signs clearly. For example, requesting a deposit before

Page 6

viewing or using the same property photos with different addresses can be written as
rules. A rule-based expert system is transparent because it can explain which rules
were triggered, which is valuable in a high-trust area like housing.

However, a purely symbolic system would be too limited. Scammers can change their
wording, payment requests, or posting style once they know which rules are being used.
A fixed rule list may catch obvious scams but miss more subtle ones. It may also create
false positives if rules are too rigid. A remote landlord is not automatically a scammer,
and a low price is not automatically fraud. Symbolic AI works best when knowledge can
be clearly written down, but rental scam detection also includes messy patterns in text,
images, behaviour, and market context.

A connectionist or machine-learning approach is useful because the system can learn
patterns from data. Instead of depending only on fixed rules, it can detect combinations
of features that are hard to describe manually. The exact wording of a scam message
may change, but the model may still learn that certain patterns of urgency, payment
requests, account behaviour, and copied photos are risky. Machine learning is also
useful for ranking risk rather than making a simple yes-or-no decision.

However, a purely connectionist system also has weaknesses. It may be less
explainable, especially if it uses complex neural networks. It may learn unfair or
meaningless patterns, such as treating certain neighbourhoods or writing styles as
suspicious. It may also fail when scammers change tactics or when the system is used
in a city different from the training data. Because of these risks, a pure machine-learning
approach is not ideal.

The hybrid approach is better because it combines the strengths of both methods. The
symbolic layer handles clear warning signs and produces explanations. The machine
learning layer handles flexible pattern recognition and risk scoring. The search
component helps compare a listing to nearby rentals, duplicate images, repeated
descriptions, and known scam patterns. Together, these components make the system
more practical than either approach alone.

A human-only solution would involve renters, moderators, landlords, and
consumer-protection staff manually checking listings. Renters could search the address,
compare prices, ask for a viewing, verify ownership, and avoid sending deposits early.
Platform moderators could review suspicious listings reported by users. This approach
has advantages because humans can understand context and notice unusual
explanations.

However, the human-only approach is slow and inconsistent. Many renters do not know
what to check, especially if they are new to a city or under time pressure. Platforms may
receive too many listings and reports for moderators to review quickly. Human reviewers
may also miss repeated scam patterns across different platforms or cities. RentSafe AI
has an advantage because it can screen listings earlier and at a larger scale. It can
quickly compare prices, images, wording, and account behaviour, then help humans

Page 7

focus on the highest risk cases. The best solution is not AI instead of humans, but AI
helping humans make safer decisions.

The concerns would also change under a human-only solution. Correctness and bias
would still matter because humans can also make mistakes or judge listings unfairly.
Accountability might be clearer because a human made the decision, but consistency
would be harder to maintain. A new concern would also arise around reviewer
subjectivity, since individual moderators may apply different standards depending on
personal judgment or familiarity with local rental markets. With RentSafe AI, the main
challenge is making the system explainable, testable, and reviewable. With a
human-only system, the main challenge is scale and consistency. Because rental
scams can spread quickly online, a carefully designed hybrid AI system is the stronger
option.
